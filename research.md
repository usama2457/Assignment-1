The four goals I set for myself after graduation becoming debt-free with $500,000 in combined savings and investments, reaching a lean and sustainable 200lbs through consistent training, visiting at least 10 countries, including a solo trip, and building a career I'm proud of are self-directed and personally meaningful, chosen based on what I actually want out of life rather than what a program requires me to demonstrate. This distinguishes them from typical academic learning outcomes, which are externally defined by an institution and tied to standardized, measurable criteria a student must meet regardless of personal relevance. Moeller, Theiler, and Wu (2012) describe this contrast through goal orientation theory: a task-focused orientation, intrinsically driven by learning and self-improvement, versus an ability-focused orientation, driven by external rewards like grades or outperforming others. My goals fall closer to the former: paying off loans, hitting a savings target, or training consistently aren't about being graded against a rubric; they're benchmarks I set for my own growth. Moeller et al. (2012) also found that goals designed and evaluated by students themselves tend to be more authentic and meaningful, and that students who choose their own goals generally engage more deeply than those working toward goals set for them. This idea appears in a different context in Oudkerk Pool, Jaarsma, Driessen, and Govaerts (2020), who studied medical students documenting their competence in clinical portfolios. Their research found that students often felt torn between demonstrating institutionally required competencies and documenting what they personally saw as meaningful evidence of growth, with many wishing they had more room in their portfolio to include their own perspective. This tension mirrors the difference between this assignment's personal goal-setting exercise and formal academic outcomes: while my four goals financial independence, physical health, meaningful travel, and career growth are shaped entirely by my own priorities, most coursework and learning outcomes are structured around requirements I didn't choose, which doesn't always leave room to reflect what I actually find meaningful about my own development.
